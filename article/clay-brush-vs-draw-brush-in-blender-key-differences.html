Clay Brush vs Draw Brush in Blender: Key Differences

In Blender's sculpting mode, both the Clay brush and the Draw brush are fundamental tools for building 3D geometry, but they achieve volume in vastly different ways. While the Draw brush adds or subtracts volume smoothly along the surface normal to follow existing mesh contours, the Clay brush deposits flattened, strip-like layers that build up planes and fill in gaps. Understanding the mechanical differences between these two brushes—including surface falloff, plane flattening, and volume accumulation—allows sculptors to choose the right tool for blocking out primary forms or adding refined details.

How the Draw Brush Works

The Draw brush is the default sculpting tool in Blender. It pushes vertices outward (or pulls them inward when holding Ctrl) along the direction of the surface normals, creating smooth, rounded curves.

  • Stroke Profile: Generates a soft, curved stroke with a smooth radial falloff.
  • Surface Interaction: Conforms naturally to the existing underlying curvature of the mesh, accentuating current shapes rather than flattening them.
  • Primary Uses: Ideal for adding general organic volume, drawing sharp creases, sculpting flowing forms like hair or cloth folds, and refining muscle structures during mid-to-late sculpting stages.

How the Clay Brush Works

The Clay brush mimics the behavior of traditional physical clay. Instead of pushing existing surface contours outward smoothly, it builds up flat, rectangular strips of material on top of the mesh.

  • Stroke Profile: Generates a flat, square-ish stroke profile with defined edges.
  • Surface Interaction: Actively flattens the high points of the surface while filling in the low points, creating a planar building block effect.
  • Primary Uses: Essential for blocking out primary and secondary forms, defining hard planar anatomy (like jawlines or collarbones), and filling in unwanted mesh depressions.

Direct Comparison of Key Features

1. Volume Accumulation

  • Draw Brush: Builds up continuous, dome-like peaks. Repeated strokes in the same area will stack on top of each other, creating taller rounded ridges.
  • Clay Brush: Depots flat, overlapping strips. Repeated strokes build up thick, planar layers reminiscent of applying physical clay strips with a palette knife.

2. Surface Flattening (Plane Definition)

  • Draw Brush: Offers no flattening effect. It preserves the curvature of the mesh beneath the stroke.
  • Clay Brush: Inherently flattens as it adds height, making it far superior for establishing planes, angles, and hard surface boundaries.

3. Workflow and Sculpting Stage

  • Clay Brush: Best used in the early blocking-out stages of a sculpt to establish overall proportion, silhouette, and planar structure.
  • Draw Brush: Best used throughout the entire workflow, particularly in the middle and final stages to build subtle contours, define organic flow, and lay down fine surface details.
