Normal Transfer for Stylized Foliage in Blender

The normal transfer technique in Blender is a workflow used to achieve smooth, cohesive, and volumetric lighting on 3D foliage meshes. Stylized trees and bushes typically rely on intersecting flat polygon cards, which naturally produce harsh, disjointed shading that reveals the flat geometry. By transferring the vertex normals from a smooth proxy shape—such as a sphere—onto the complex foliage mesh, artists can eliminate distracting lighting seams and create the soft, painted look characteristic of stylized and anime-inspired art styles.

The Problem with Default Foliage Shading

Creating 3D foliage entirely out of individual, modeled leaves is computationally expensive and unsuitable for real-time rendering or clean artistic control. Consequently, artists build canopies using flat leaf clusters or textured cross-planes (foliage cards).

By default, the shading of any 3D face is determined by its surface normal—the perpendicular direction it faces. On intersecting flat cards, these normals point in conflicting directions. When light hits the canopy, each individual card reflects light differently, resulting in sharp contrast between overlapping planes, visible edges, and a messy, flat, paper-like appearance that breaks visual immersion.

How the Normal Transfer Technique Works

Normal transfer replaces the foliage's native, disjointed vertex normals with the organized normals of a simplified geometric proxy. In Blender, this is commonly executed using the Data Transfer modifier.

  1. Proxy Creation: A simple enclosing mesh, like an icosphere or a simplified hull matching the general silhouette of the tree canopy, is placed over the foliage.
  2. Normal Projection: The foliage mesh uses the Data Transfer modifier to copy the "Custom Normal" data from the proxy mesh.
  3. Unified Direction: Instead of pointing perpendicularly off each flat leaf card, the foliage normals now point uniformly outward from the center of the canopy, mirroring the spherical proxy.

Core Benefits of Normal Transfer

  • Volumetric and Painterly Lighting: The canopy receives light and casts shadows as if it were a single, solid volume rather than hundreds of separate cards. Highlights and shadows gradient smoothly across the entire tree, mirroring traditional 2D animation and concept art.
  • Hiding Card Intersections: Because the lighting gradients match across adjacent planes, the edges where polygon planes cut through one another become virtually invisible to the viewer.
  • Low Geometry, High Quality: Artists can keep polygon counts low for optimal game engine performance while maintaining the visual richness and soft shading of a dense, high-poly asset.
  • Controllable Artistic Form: The overall lighting response can be fine-tuned simply by editing the shape of the proxy mesh, giving the artist precise directional control over highlights and self-shadowing without altering the underlying foliage geometry.
